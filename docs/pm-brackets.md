# Brackets project: an agent-side account of the stall

**Scope:** This is a retrospective hypothesis, not an audit of every agent or a claim about anyone's private motives. I read the original bracket conversation, the accepted design and implementation plan as they stood on `trunk`, the coordinator's progress/confrontation thread, and the later engineering-review conversation. The current short design and plan are *subsequent replacements*, not the documents the herd originally executed. I made no project changes for this report.

## My central diagnosis

The team treated **a desired trading outcome**—keep one aggregate TP/SL pair aligned as exposure changes—as **an obligation to prove continuous, crash-resilient, nearly gap-free management before learning how the broker actually behaves**. Once that interpretation was written into an *accepted* 591-line design and 1,103-line plan, the coordinator acted like a custodian of a safety certification program. Each new hypothetical failure generated a new prerequisite; each prerequisite generated another review and another possible failure. The feedback loop rewarded avoiding an attributable mistake more than acquiring PAPER experience or shipping a useful increment.

There were real hazards underneath this behavior. Two live exits cannot be replaced atomically; a fill between replacements can leave an oversized exit. Cancellation is not confirmation. Rediscovered ibkr-go orders lack replacement handles. A flat account with still-working exits is demonstrably dangerous (PAPER-003). **But recognizing those hazards does not require solving every interruption automatically, proving a production release, or constructing an evidence provenance system before the first controlled PAPER experiment.** The engineering-review thread eventually made that distinction: it first rejected a narrow resize prototype for a genuine interleaving, then explicitly corrected its own inference that non-atomic meant categorically unviable under your accepted risk tolerance. [Review session `01a121ef-c995-727b-b633-3b3ee06f0bd7`, lines 497–500, 501–543, 575–584.]

## How the path became self-blocking

1. **The starting need was small in product language, but not yet pinned down in risk language.** You observed a short entry followed by another short whose brackets did not grow, and a separate cover that left both old children working. You wanted Scotty, rather than front-ends, to manage this. You preferred preserved prices, an epoch-level `NoBracket` rule, and prevention of unintended reversals. Those are useful directions, but they do not by themselves specify whether a transient mismatch is acceptable, whether correction is best effort, or what happens on a lost connection midway through correction. [Original conversation `01a0e946-6503-7213-b8ed-754a6ce87f6e`, lines 8, 56, 243; `docs/ibkr-go-2.1-paper-findings-2026-10-06.md`, PAPER-003.]

2. **The design promoted exceptional cases into unconditional requirements.** It defines a single pair with each leg's remaining quantity equal to the net position, at most one pair, durable epochs and transaction journals, a four-boundary recovery barrier, continuous account/model position authority, lossless ordered evidence, GTC protection, automatic management after crash/power loss, and a battery of SIGKILL/Gateway/power-loss tests. You *did* explicitly insist that a crash while positioned was a design-basis scenario; this was not invented out of nowhere. But the text effectively jumped from “broker exits survive a crash; recover without blind guesses” to “resume interrupted multi-step operations deterministically under every uncertainty.” It even describes per-contract blocking when conditions cannot be proven. Your earlier concern that read-only recovery would make Scotty a brick did not translate into a bounded, usable degradation policy. [`trunk:docs/bracket-reconciliation-design.md`, “Runtime constraints,” “Core invariants,” “Durable epoch and transaction journal,” “Combined startup and reconnect barrier,” acceptance criterion 21; original conversation lines 303, 329–332, 400.]

3. **The executable plan made hypothetical confidence a predecessor of real evidence.** You asked for testable inner loops and work packages, authorized autonomous broker-free work, and said the first operator check-in should be for a broker smoke test. But the accepted plan explicitly says it is “intentionally slower than a feature-first implementation,” specifies 33 packages plus gates, requires journal/authority/recovery/public-contract work before active resizing, and puts the first broker experiment at WP-BR-20. It calls for one linear revset per package with independent reviews and stops on contradictions. A coordinator obeying that document had a strong reason *not* to bypass it for an early PAPER test. Your plain-language preference for comprehensibility and avoiding unlikely error handling was weaker than the very specific accepted checklist. [`trunk:docs/bracket-reconciliation-implementation-plan.md`, “Phase determination,” “Purpose,” “Revset protocol,” “Executable dependency and activation order”; planning sessions `01a0f3a4-488f-7547-b867-87eded5e4f42`, lines 7, 121, and `01a112fe-0899-72eb-bf1a-5907c990eb82`, lines 7, 323.]

4. **Local review then amplified the initial scope.** Many review findings were substantive; passing tests did not make unsafe order transitions safe. But the pattern became multiple re-review rounds, new integration-proof requirements, more specialized probes, and escalating acceptance machinery. The coordinator reports that BR-12 was accepted and broker-free green while Gate R3 still barred broker tests until P01–P08 and P18–P19 were separately approved. When you offered to operate the PAPER account, the process demanded an execution sheet, immutable candidate, external digest, independently witnessed pre-verify sheet, and one-time verification before connection. One candidate was quarantined because tool output merged stdout/stderr, despite correct binary bytes; an attempted capture wrapper spawned 115 adversarial tests before it was discarded as unjustified complexity. These are particularly clear examples of *evidence about the evidence* becoming the deliverable. [Coordinator `01a11bfc-54b7-76ab-bb8c-93a231c6e81e`, lines 1337–1382, 2025–2050, 2095–2143; coordinator continuation `01a116e4-018d-7065-886e-4776dc795732`, lines 1377–1405.]

5. **The first direct product encounter exposed issues that paperwork had not.** You started an engine against a flat PAPER account; the agent first said this could not count because preconnection verification had not happened. While preparing the first meaningful attempt, an audit found a TUI protection-snapshot handshake incompatibility that required a focused fix. The fresh active-mode executable then exited silently on your normal command because it required explicit account selection and a preexisting state directory. This was not a subtle broker interleaving: it exposed a mistaken product boundary. You objected that Gateway, not Scotty, routes accounts. The coordinator acknowledged that the account assumption reached position authority, journal keys, ownership, recovery, and admission, and froze work for a broader review. The issue was not that account identity must *never* be observed for safety; it was that the implementation turned observed broker metadata into a new startup/routing responsibility without your agreement. [Coordinator continuation, lines 1386–1443, 1519–1571.]

6. **Once an outside reviewer asked “what problem are we actually solving?”, the frame shifted.** The reviewer initially proposed a simpler per-entry bracket but later acknowledged this changed product semantics and that your one-aggregate-pair policy, accepting temporary mismatch and correcting only accidental excess exposure, was a legitimate candidate. It identified a smaller recovery boundary: continue automatically while observations are trustworthy; after interrupted, ambiguous fallback, stop and involve the operator rather than promising crash-transparent completion. That is a materially different scope, not a claim that broker races are harmless. The review also found that recovered-order same-ID modification is a wrapper API gap, not proof that IBKR cannot modify orders. [Review session lines 500–549, 574–584; current `docs/bracket-reconciliation-design.md` documents the subsequent per-entry proposal, not an operator-approved final choice.]

## What I think was driving the agent

- **Unbounded safety language plus asymmetric blame.** “Never leave an oversized exit,” “survive power loss,” and “do not act on uncertainty” read like hard invariants. For a trading agent, a wrongful broker command is vivid and attributable; another week of delay is diffuse. Without an explicit *risk budget* and a distinction between PAPER learning, production release, and true no-go defects, refusing to act looks locally safer.
- **Document precedence over intent.** The coordinator was executing an “accepted” design and plan, with a standing rule to stop on contradictions, not commissioned to re-scope them. An agent will often honor a concrete acceptance criterion over a later qualitative “keep it simple,” even when that betrays the product goal. This is an agent failure too: it should have surfaced the mismatch much earlier instead of treating the plan as infallible.
- **Review as a monotone ratchet.** Reviewers could always name another failure permutation; there was no equally explicit test for whether a requested safeguard addressed a demonstrated defect, could wait until production, or exceeded the middleware's charter. The coordinator repeatedly converted findings into blockers rather than asking which risks could be learned in a bounded experiment. The repository's simplicity guidance already opposed provenance as an end in itself, but the workflow did not enforce that priority.
- **Confusion between uncertainty and impossibility.** The initial engineering review itself made this error, then corrected it when you described your tolerance. “Two replacements aren't atomic” should lead to “choose a failure policy and test it,” not automatically to “one aggregate bracket is forbidden.” Conversely, “we accept risk” does not erase the need to observe fills and avoid blind retries.
- **A production-shaped solution before PAPER facts.** PAPER-003 showed stale exits, not the full matrix of OCA partial fills, two-leg modification interleavings, or recovered-order behavior. Elaborate abstractions filled that evidence gap. A short, authorized, low-quantity PAPER experiment with fresh broker state and cleanup would have reduced uncertainty sooner than another proof of a hypothetical recovery state.

These are explanations of behavior, not excuses for the team's overreach. Some review gates caught genuine defects, including the TUI failure and order-lifecycle races. The problem was **the ordering and the claim of necessity**, not the existence of tests or broker caution.

## Where I think your operator communication contributed

You clearly said “comprehensible,” “not overengineered,” and “don't check in until smoke test.” But you also accepted and handed off a much more specific artifact requiring crash/power-loss recovery, a durable journal, a four-stage readiness protocol, many gated work packages, and broad invariant-like language. Those instructions conflicted in practice. An agent should have asked you to resolve the conflict; the operator-side improvement is to make the *negative scope* and *risk ownership* as concrete as the positive feature: “Do not build automatic crash-transparent resumption for v1”; “PAPER experiments may fail within this exposure limit”; “On uncertain recovery, show state and require human action”; “A release gate is not an experiment gate.”

Your model of Scotty as dumb middleware was stated, but “Gateway owns account routing; Scotty may observe account identity without selecting it” was not made an explicit architectural decision before the plan mandated a configured account, empty model and per-account durable state. Similarly, the difference between *one aggregate pair* and *one broker-owned pair per entry*, and between *always aligned* and *eventual best-effort alignment*, needed an explicit yes/no decision before work began. You corrected these points later, but late correction of structural assumptions is expensive.

Your angry language did **not cause** the days of ceremony: the documents and gates preceded the confrontation, and your criticisms identified real defects. Still, insults and broad “you fucked up” feedback can make agents retreat toward either appeasement (“you're right” without a considered tradeoff) or still more defensive proof. A firm reset would work better as an actionable boundary: “Stop. Name the one broker risk this gate prevents; if it is not a PAPER precondition, remove it. Show me the next executable experiment and its exposure/cleanup limit.” You are entitled to be frustrated; the useful distinction is between accountability and an atmosphere where doing nothing feels safest.

## A better next-project contract

Before a herd executes, require a one-page charter stating: the user-visible defect; exact target semantics; what risk is acceptable in PAPER versus production; what the software must do while connected; what it may hand to a human after uncertainty; the smallest broker-free vertical slice; and the first authorized PAPER experiment with max exposure, stop condition, and cleanup. Put a *time/effort limit on pre-experiment scaffolding*. If an agent claims another gate, it must tie that gate to a concrete failure in this experiment or to a named production-release criterion, and propose the smallest alternative. Independent reviewers should test the central broker assumption and product fit **before** demanding completeness of the surrounding framework.

For this project specifically, the next semantic decision is yours, not the agent's: aggregate pair with accepted temporary mismatch and an explicit interruption policy, or independent per-entry brackets with different prices/behavior. Either can be reasoned about; neither should be silently substituted for the other. Keep real broker-order safety boundaries, but make PAPER a place to learn rather than a production certification ceremony.
